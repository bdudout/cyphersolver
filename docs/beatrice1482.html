Pozsony → Ferrara · graphic cipher inside clear letters · May–June 1482 · existing decipherment

Beatrice d’Aragona to Eleonora and Matthias Corvinus to Ercole I d’Este, Pozsony, May–June 1482

→

Go to the full text ↓

Two letters of the Queen of Hungary to her sister at Ferrara and one of the King to the Duke, written in ten days in one cipher. Their cipher passages were printed deciphered in 1877; the prints were matched to the manuscript images, the key was later rebuilt from the queen’s letter of 31 May, its clear copy of the time and the 1877 print, and its two disputed values were then checked on the king’s letter.

At a glanceExisting decipherment · complete

The letters
Two letters from Beatrice of Aragon, Queen of Hungary, to her sister Eleonora at Ferrara, 31 May and 8 June 1482 (DECODE R1140 and R1141; split from catalogue item 160, which grouped five records), and one from King Matthias to Duke Ercole I, 1 June 1482 (R1156), which had its own page until 14 November 2026.
Where
Archivio di Stato di Modena, Carteggio Principi Esteri: R1140 is 1623, b. 2/2, 2, 4a; R1141 is given as Cancelleria 1523 (the record name says 1623), b. 2/2, 2, 5a; R1156 is 1622, b. 1/3, 2, 2a. One page each.
How it was read
Not re-solved: the printed correspondence settles it. The queen’s letters are nos. 10 and 12 in Magyar Diplomácziai Emlékek III (1877), pp. 13–14 and 16–17, with the cipher passages set in italics; the king’s was printed there and again by Fraknói in 1895 (no. 131). The king’s letter was also read again from its signs.
The key
Rebuilt on 14 November 2026 by Brice Dudout (PR #620, with AI agents) from the letter of 31 May, aligned on its clear copy of the time and the print, then measured on the letter of 8 June, kept aside: 0.900 of the characters of the printed text, against 0.290 for the best of 200 shuffled keys. Partial: three shapes have no value. The king’s letter is in the same key: on its image the figure 9 is d and the figure 2 is g, as in the queen’s letters.
What it says
Matthias offers an intervention against Venice with ten thousand Swiss and six thousand cavalry, provided the league sends money to Milan; in his own letter he has five hundred picked horse ready for Ferrara and no safe road to send them by.
Still open
Words the printed decipherment (as read from OCR) leaves uncertain are marked [?] in the queen’s readings; three shapes of the key have no value; two large signs after regina in the king’s letter are unread, and a few of its single signs are doubtful on the web image; whether its L-shaped sign is a second sign for g or the sign for s, and whether the queen’s table had the further signs of the king’s letter, are not settled; the 2010 edition that Vestigia lists for the letter of 8 June and the 1877 print of the king’s letter have not been opened.

The queen’s two letters are to her sister Eleonora d’Aragona, Duchess of Ferrara; the king’s is to Eleonora’s husband, Duke Ercole I. DECODE gives R1141’s holder as “Cancelleria, 1523”, its record name says 1623. The king’s letter is Latin; DECODE lists it as Italian.

02 31 May 1482: troops against Venice

The edition gives the plaintext of the cipher: Matthias offers an intervention against Venice, provided the league sends money to Milan. The proposed force comprises ten thousand Swiss and six thousand cavalry; five hundred more cavalry, including a hundred hussars, also need a safe route. He does not intend to declare himself openly against Venice before his relationship with the league is settled.

uno expediente optimo et necessario per lo favore et salute de tuta la lega

Dece milia Svizari et sei milia cavalli del Signor Re di Hungaria.

These are excerpts of the 1877 decipherment. The manuscript’s cleartext and cipher boundaries match the edition’s roman and italic sections. The editorial note states that the italic words were written in secret signs.

03 8 June 1482: money and effective cooperation

Beatrice relays Matthias’s criticism of the league’s reluctance to spend. The king repeats the troop offer, requires payment at Milan, and asks for decisive cooperation. The published text has the proverb:

ali avari che per sparagnare uno grosso perdeno lo ducato

Misers save a grosso and lose a ducat. Near the end the decipherment describes endless negotiations as “uno Jocare de balla cum retornare sempre ali principij”—playing ball and always returning to the starting point. The printed page has balla; the OCR text gave holla. The edition and manuscript both date the letter 8 June, despite a secondary citation giving July.

The 1877 print leaves out one cipher run of this letter. It prints non solamente havia tolto, ma li haria ridocti a carastia de defenderle. The clear copy made at the time adds the missing words in its margin, and the rebuilt key decodes the run the same way: non solamente havia tolto Venetiani da sua offesa, ma li haria ridocti a carastia de defenderse: the league’s money would not only have kept the Venetians from attacking, it would have left them short of means to defend themselves. The reading and the translation below carry the correction (Brice Dudout, 14 November 2026). It is new against the 1877 print; the 2010 edition that Vestigia lists for this letter was not opened.

04 1 June 1482: the king’s letter

The day after the queen’s first letter, Matthias wrote to Duke Ercole himself, in Latin. Venice had opened the war on Ferrara in May; Matthias was at war with the Emperor Frederick III, whose lands lay between Hungary and Italy. The courtesies and the reasoning are in clear. The numbers, the troops, the route and the names are in cipher, set into the clear lines as short runs: 69 runs and 409 signs, ten of them signs for names and two more large signs that are not read.

… statim cum domina regina consorte nostra statuimus [mittere] in [subsidium vestrum quingentos electos equites], inter [quos] essent [centum usarones], idest [homines] levis [armature], qui plus [ceteris valent]. Sed cum examinaremus cum domina regina ‹two signs› et [aliis] nostris, per [quam viam] possemus [eos mittere], non invenimus aliquam [tutam]. …

In crastinum hinc recedet [Mager Balas capitaneus noster] cum [pluribus gentibus], iturus in [Croatiam] versus [confinia Venetorum], ubi [plures] etiam [congregabit]; et quotienscunque inventa erit [tuta transeundi via], statim significet [Regi Hungarie] et illic faciet quod [venient].

Speramus cito nos [res nostras] ita [disposituros], ut meliori postea modo [vos iuvare] possemus. [Rex Hungarie scripsit regi Ferdinando] quomodo possent [diverti Veneti] et [confundere eos] et cum paucis [pecuniis].

“… at once, with our lady the queen, we decided to send five hundred picked horse to your aid, among them a hundred hussars, that is light horse, who are worth more than the rest. But when we considered with the queen and our other counsellors by what road we could send them, we found none safe. … Tomorrow our captain Balázs Magyar leaves here with a large force for Croatia, towards the Venetian border, where he will gather more; and whenever a safe way through is found, he is to tell the King of Hungary at once, and will see to it that they come there. We hope soon to have our own affairs so arranged that afterwards we can help you better. The King of Hungary has written to King Ferdinand how the Venetians might be drawn off and thrown into confusion, and at small cost.”

Fraknói’s text of 1895 goes wrong in the cipher runs in several places. The letter was first read from its signs here on 19 September 2026, from the DECODE images, and four of these places were set right. On 14 November 2026 a clear copy of the letter made at the time was found on Vestigia (document 2627, beside the cipher original, 2626), and the runs were transcribed sign by sign from Vestigia’s image with the copy next to them. The copy shows what the decipherer of the time had:

  • “qui plus ceteris valoris (így)” in the print. The run is v-a-l-e-n-t, and the copy has valent.
  • quingentos electos equites. The word electos, seven signs at the head of line 9, is in the cipher and in the copy. The 1895 print lacks it, and so did the reading made here in September.
  • “cum pluribus servis” in the print. The signs spell s-e-n-t-i-b-u-s, and the copy reads sentibus, which is no word. gentibus is by the sense: Balázs leaves with troops. The same L-shaped sign stands where g is wanted in congregabit and excogitare, so it may be a second sign for g that the decipherer took for s.
  • “Speramus nos eos nostris contra dispositiones ut meliori postea modo possemus (így)” in the print. The copy has what the signs spell: Speramus cito nos res nostras ita disposituros, ut meliori postea modo vos iuvare possemus.
  • The “regest”. Fraknói prints the last sentence, Rex Hungarie scripsit regi Ferdinando, quomodo possent diverti Veneti et confundere eos et cum paucis pecuniis, as a note in a hand of the time at the end of the letter. It is the letter’s own last sentence, half in cipher, before the date; the copy has it in the same place.
  • illic faciet quod venient. The print has convenire. The copy reads significet Regi Hungarie et illic faciet quod venient: the sign after significet is the king’s name sign, and what follows faciet is the ordinary abbreviation of quod in clear, not a cipher sign as was thought here before.

So three of the four September corrections to the 1895 print (valent, the Speramus sentence and the closing sentence as part of the letter) are right, but they are corrections to the print, not new readings: the copy of the time already had them. The fourth, gentibus, is not in the copy, which reads sentibus; it rests on the sense. It is not known whether the 1877 print has any of them, since the letter was taken here from Fraknói’s text only. Two large signs after cum domina regina have nothing to match in the copy and are not read. A few single signs are doubtful on the web image: a small z-like sign for i in mittere, two shapes for rr in terras, and two strokes that stand inside congregabit and disposituros with no letter to match.

05 What was verified

The text of the three letters is the existing decipherment in print (1877, and 1895 for the king’s): the printed decipherments were found and matched to the images by dates, salutations, clear passages and cipher boundaries. Until 14 November 2026 there was no transcription of the signs of any of them, and no key for the queen’s letters; the king’s letter had a working key drawn up from the DECODE images in September.

The key, from the clear copy and the print. The Vestigia database publishes each letter twice: the cipher original and a clear copy made at the time (documents 1957 and 1958 for 31 May, 1960 and 1961 for 8 June). Brice Dudout (PR #620, with AI agents) had the signs of both originals transcribed by shape alone, 852 signs for 31 May and 648 for 8 June, and rebuilt the key from the letter of 31 May aligned on its clear copy and on the 1877 print. The alignment started from a table that already held 9 = d and 2 = g; re-run at integration without them, the letter of 31 May gives the same two values. The protocol and the key were fixed first and the key was then measured once on the whole letter of 8 June, by the contributor’s record for the order of events. It gives 0.900 of the characters of the printed text (0.644 of the words), against 0.290 for the best of 200 keys with their values shuffled; the bar set beforehand was 0.85 and 0.30 above the controls. The measurement was run again at integration with the same figures, 62 signs of the letter of 8 June were compared with Vestigia’s image and agree, and the margin of the clear copy was checked on the image.

The cipher is a substitution of invented signs and figures for single letters; in the queen’s letters it has a second sign for a few letters (a, o, u, n), signs for doubled letters (tt, ss, ff, ll) and two signs for names, the King of Hungary and the Venetians. The key is partial: three shapes that occur eight times in the letter of 8 June have no value, and two values were taken from the key of the king’s letter. The method for the key is key recovered from external plaintext; it changes nothing in the text, which was in print.

One key for the queen and the king. The working key of the king’s letter and the queen’s key agreed on sixteen letter values (a, b, c, h, i, l, m, n, o, p, q, r, s, t, u and the sign for tt) and on the sign for the King of Hungary, and differed on three letters and one name. The king’s key had the figure 2 for d, a hooked L for g, 5, 4, 9 and another sign for e, and a looped & for the Venetians; the queen’s has 9 for d, 2 for g, one sign for e, and a cross with a small circle for the Venetians. Brice Dudout proposed that the “2” of the king’s key was the queen’s 9. The places were looked at again here, by the project, on Vestigia’s image of the king’s letter on 14 November 2026:

Decoded with the queen’s key and the signs that only this letter has, the 397 signs that are not names give 389 of the 399 letters of the clear copy (0.975); with the king’s old values for 9 and 2 they give 379, losing each of the ten places with d or g (in two of them, modum, the sign is read as d from the copy, its head being unclear). The queen’s key as it stands, built on her letter of 31 May alone, gives 283 (0.709): it has no value for the signs her letters do not use. This check was made on a transcription done with the clear copy in view, and the signs that only this letter has were given their values from this letter and its copy; it is not a test on text kept aside, and no controls were run. What it does show is the ten places with d or g: all ten go to the queen’s values. The three letters are therefore taken to be in one key. The king’s secretary uses more of it than the queen’s: two more signs for e, a second sign for i and for s, a further one for u, and name signs for King Ferdinand, the Duke of Ferrara and the Emperor. Whether the queen’s copy of the table had these signs is not known: it is not excluded that hers was a shorter copy of the king’s. The king’s letter had its own page until this was settled; it is now part of this one. The 1482 key does not read the letter of 8 March 1486 (its page).

Tomokiyo’s Cryptiana index has no article on Beatrice; his other publications were not searched. Pozsony, 31 May 1482

Beatrice of Aragon writes to her sister Eleonora, Duchess of Ferrara, during the War of Ferrara: for 100,000 ducats sent to Milan, King Matthias would raise 16,000 men (10,000 Swiss and 6,000 cavalry) in the Brescia and Verona districts against Venice, but will not declare himself openly before his relations with the league are settled. Decryption: uncorrected OCR of the printed decipherment in Magyar Diplomácziai Emlékek III (1877) no. 10.

# R1140, Beatrice of Aragon to Eleonora, Duchess of Ferrara, Pozsony, 31 May 1482: English translation

Most Illustrious Lady, most honoured Sister, after due recommendation. I am writing more fully on behalf of His Majesty
the King my consort to His Majesty the Lord King our father about the matters below, for fear these letters might
miscarry, the road being dangerous; and I shall leave long words aside and come to the conclusion. Having understood the
danger you are in, the Lord King of Hungary, notwithstanding the great business he has in being at war on two sides, one
against the Turk, the other against the attack that the Emperor with the whole Empire seeks to make on him, has
nevertheless decided on an excellent and necessary expedient for the favour and safety of the whole league. His Majesty,
after he understood the preparations of the Pope and of the Venetians against the league, and above all against your
state, sent to the Swiss, who are his confederates, and an answer has come with some captains of the said Swiss, and [?]
and the matter arranged, that if the league is willing only to spend a hundred thousand ducats and send them to Milan,
and the Lord King of Hungary to bear the rest of the expense, and that it be soon, he will bring it about that by the end
of the month of August there will be sixteen thousand fighting men in the territories of Brescia and Verona against the
Venetians: ten thousand Swiss and six thousand horse of the Lord King of Hungary. Furthermore His Majesty holds 500 horse
ready to send you, among them a hundred hussars; but he must be told by what route he is to send them to you: you must
send us a commissary of yours. Besides that, the Lord King of Hungary is sending Megrebal [Magyar Balázs] to the Croatian
border to raise men, likewise in your favour; but he declares to you that, not being otherwise considered in your
league, he does not wish to declare himself openly an enemy of the Venetians, but that the said Swiss should make war as
soldiers of the league, and he would gladly have Don Federico as their captain; and when the King of Hungary has entered
your league, he intends to show himself openly an enemy of the Venetians. And beyond this he further offers that, if the
league will help him with only a hundred thousand ducats more, His Majesty will spend on his side a much greater sum, and
will raise another army and fall upon the Venetians from the other side, so that everyone would see that the way would
be good. So that when the league is minded in earnest to help itself, it will find the right way not only to divert the
Venetians from attacking it, but not to be attacked by them.

Encourage the Most Illustrious Lord Duke to do his diligence and duty about the above, and meanwhile to resist
steadfastly; for, as I said above, when the league is willing to make the said expense, whatever it wishes will be easy
for it. The Lord King of Hungary is willing to put in much more than as much again, to make the Venetians feel more
damaged than they think to damage others. All I write above is by the will and order of the Most Serene Lord King my
consort and your brother, with compassionate and affectionate help, and to put it into effective execution. Pozsony, the
last of May 1482.

Your Most Illustrious Ladyship's most devoted and obedient sister, the Queen of Hungary.

To the Most Illustrious Lady Eleonora, Duchess of Ferrara etc., Lady and most honoured sister.

Pozsony, 8 June 1482

Uncertain reading (copied verbatim from NOTES.md):

Uncertain: "illic faciet ꝗ venient". The printed text has "convenire". The glyphs read ꝗ + v-e-n-i-e-n-t, so ꝗ may
here stand for *quod* or *con-*. The clear verb before the first ꝗ looks like *sufficietur*, which the printed text
gives as *significet*. Neither point changes the sense: Balázs is to tell the king as soon as a safe road is found,
and the troops are to meet there.

## Translation

Matthias, by the grace of God King of Hungary, Bohemia etc., to the most illustrious prince, Lord Hercules, Duke of
Ferrara and Modena etc., greeting and success according to his wishes. From the letters of the most illustrious Lady
Duchess, your consort, our dearest sister and sister-in-law, we have learned the state of your affairs and the
preparations of the Venetians against you, which we did not hear gladly; [and] that the most serene lord, our king
and our dearest father, and your other lords and confederates are to help you, and that they have already in fact
begun; we too, out of our singular goodwill towards you, shall not fail to do those things which we know will be of
use to you. For, having read the letters, we at once resolved with the Lady Queen our consort ⟨to send⟩ to ⟨your aid
five hundred picked horse⟩, among ⟨whom⟩ there would be ⟨a hundred hussars⟩, that is, ⟨men⟩ of light ⟨armour⟩, who
⟨are worth⟩ more ⟨than the others⟩. But when we examined with the Lady Queen [2 signs] and ⟨others⟩ of ours by ⟨what
road⟩ we could ⟨send them⟩, we found none ⟨safe⟩. In our judgement they would have ⟨to pass⟩ through ⟨the lands⟩ [of
the Emperor], with whom we ⟨are⟩ at present ⟨in conflict⟩; but of this we would not care much, for we would well find
⟨a safe way of passing⟩; but since they would have ⟨to cross⟩ ⟨the lands⟩ [of the Venetians], we fear that they could
in no way ⟨cross⟩. ⟨We have written⟩ [to King Ferdinand] that [the King himself] or [the Duke of Ferrara] might
⟨devise⟩ some ⟨way⟩ by which they could ⟨be sent safely⟩; we would gladly ⟨send them⟩. We do not doubt that they would
serve faithfully and well. Tomorrow ⟨Magyar Balázs, our captain⟩, will leave here with ⟨many troops⟩, to go into
⟨Croatia⟩ towards the ⟨borders⟩ [of the Venetians], where he will also ⟨gather⟩ ⟨more⟩; and as soon as a ⟨safe road for
passing⟩ is found, he is to make it known at once [to the King of Hungary], and will bring it about there that ⟨they
come⟩. We hope soon ⟨to arrange our affairs⟩ so that afterwards we may be able ⟨to help you⟩ in a better manner.

[The King of Hungary] ⟨has written⟩ [to King Ferdinand] how [the Venetians] might ⟨be diverted⟩ and ⟨confounded⟩, and
with little ⟨money⟩. Given at Pozsony, the first of June 1482.
